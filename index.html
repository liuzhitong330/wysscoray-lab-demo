The lab showed that aging is written into the blood. Across the human lifespan, hundreds of plasma proteins quietly rise or fall, some in distinct waves, and this signature is strong enough to read a person's age from a blood draw. It also carries factors that can age or rejuvenate the brain, which is why the lab studies young blood as a possible source of rejuvenation.

I built a small tool over the lab's data so you can look up any of these proteins and see how its level actually moves with age, across 171 people aged 21 to 107.

How it works

Each protein was measured in the plasma of every person in the study. Type a protein below and the chart plots its level against the person's age, one dot per person, with the trend line and the per-year change from the lab's model. A line sloping up means the protein rises as we get older.

The proteins that move most with age are a mix of inflammation and growth signals. Several, like GDF15 and the chemokines, are the same proteins that show up in the lab's aging clocks and in its work on how blood communicates with the brain. Being able to pull up any one of them and see its real trajectory turns a long table of coefficients into something you can actually read.

Why this matters for the lab

A per-protein aging readout is the building block behind the plasma clocks and organ-age work the lab is known for. A tool like this makes it quick to sanity-check a candidate, compare it across ages and sexes, and decide whether it is worth following into the brain. The same interface extends naturally to the lab's organ-specific aging signatures and to disease cohorts.

What I would bring

My background is in computational aging biology and proteomics. I am comfortable with high-dimensional plasma and tissue data, aging models and clocks, and building interactive tools that make a large dataset explorable. I would be glad to help with the lab's proteomic aging work and to connect it to brain and disease outcomes.

Methods note

Data: Lehallier et al., Nature Medicine 2019 (Wyss-Coray lab), Supplementary Table 16, SomaScan plasma proteomics of 171 individuals aged 21 to 107, with per-protein age models from Supplementary Table 4. The chart shows normalized log-scale protein levels versus age; the trend line is a simple linear fit and the per-year change is the model's age coefficient. About sixty of the most age-associated and best-known proteins are included here for the explorer. This is an association across a cross-section of ages, not a within-person trajectory.
